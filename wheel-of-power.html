Wheel of Power

About this activity

The Wheel of Power is a reflective tool that helps you think about how different aspects of identity and social position can shape people's experiences. It brings together dimensions such as gender, race and ethnicity, sexuality, education, wealth, health and citizenship, and invites you to consider how these may influence opportunities, expectations and barriers within society.

The wheel is based on ideas from social theory, including privilege, marginalisation and intersectionality. These concepts suggest that people's experiences are shaped not by a single characteristic, but by the interaction of multiple dimensions of identity and social position.

The centre of the wheel represents positions that are more likely to align with dominant social norms and institutions. The edge represents positions that are more likely to experience exclusion, disadvantage or marginalisation. Most people will find that some of their positions sit closer to the centre while others sit closer to the edge.

This activity is not a test and does not measure your worth, character, success or contribution. It cannot capture the full complexity of a person's life. Instead, it is designed to support reflection and discussion about how social structures can create advantages for some groups and barriers for others.

There are no right or wrong answers. You may disagree with aspects of the wheel, place yourself differently from other people, or feel that your experiences are not fully represented. Those responses are part of the learning process. The aim is not to reach a single correct interpretation, but to think critically about how identity, power and inequality operate in different contexts.

Place a dot in each section of the wheel to show where you think you sit. The centre represents positions that are more likely to align with dominant social norms and institutions. The edge represents positions that are more likely to experience marginalisation or exclusion. As you place your dots, a pattern will form across the wheel, helping you reflect on how different dimensions of identity and social position interact.

How the numbers work

The percentages used in this activity are a teaching tool. They are not objective measurements of a person, identity or experience.

The scoring was designed for this activity and is not taken from a published method. The interpretation of the scores is based on the work of Ange-Marie Hancock (2007), Lisa Bowleg (2008) and Greta Bauer (2014), who examine how different dimensions of identity and social position interact.

Each dot represents a position along one dimension, measured as a distance from the centre of the wheel. The activity interprets these positions in two different ways.

The positional score calculates an overall score by combining all dimensions. The anchor dimensions count double. This approach treats each dimension as a separate factor.

The intersectional score assumes that dimensions influence one another. A person may hold advantages in some areas while experiencing marginalisation in others. The intersectional score therefore examines how different dimensions interact rather than considering them independently.

For example, a person may hold advantages associated with wealth or education while also occupying positions that are further from the social norm on other dimensions. The interaction between these positions may shape how those advantages are experienced.

When several anchor dimensions sit further from the centre, their combined effect becomes more significant. Hancock describes this as a limitation of approaches that simply add categories together. Social experiences are often shaped by interactions between dimensions, not by individual dimensions alone.

Bowleg makes a related critique. Any approach that reduces identities to separate categories risks overlooking how those categories are lived together. The two scores are presented side by side so that you can compare them and consider the assumptions behind each approach.

Versions of the wheel
Canadian Council for Refugees
An anti-oppression wheel later adapted by Sylvia Duckworth.
Sylvia Duckworth (2020)
A twelve-dimension version including citizenship, skin colour, formal education, ability, sexuality, neurodiversity, mental health, body size, housing, wealth, language and gender.
Open Oregon Educational Resources (Puttman, Willi Hooper and Antrosiglio)
A sixteen-dimension version that adds race and ethnicity, indigeneity, geography and marital status. It also identifies forms of oppression associated with each dimension. This activity is based on that version.
FORRT Academic Wheel of Privilege
A version designed for academic contexts, developed through work on neurodiversity, open scholarship and equity in higher education.
